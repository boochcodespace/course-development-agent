# SOLO Taxonomy (Biggs & Collis, 1982)

## Origin

The Structure of Observed Learning Outcomes (SOLO) taxonomy was developed by John B. Biggs and Kevin F. Collis, published in *Evaluating the Quality of Learning: The SOLO Taxonomy* (Academic Press, 1982). It was later expanded in Biggs & Tang's *Teaching for Quality Learning at University* (3rd ed., 2007).

Where Bloom's taxonomy categorizes the **type** of cognitive process and Webb's DOK measures the **depth of complexity**, SOLO describes the **structural quality and integration of a learner's response** — how well a learner has organized their understanding into a coherent whole. SOLO was built by carefully analyzing actual student responses to assessment tasks and identifying recurring structural patterns in how understanding develops.

The central insight: understanding is not simply a matter of knowing more facts. It is a matter of how well those facts are **connected, integrated, and generalized**. A learner who can recite ten supply chain terms has not necessarily achieved relational understanding of the supply chain.

---

## The five levels

SOLO describes two broad types of learning:

- **Surface learning** (Prestructural → Unistructural → Multistructural): quantity of isolated information increases
- **Deep learning** (Relational → Extended Abstract): quality of integration and transferability increases

The jump from Multistructural to Relational is **qualitative**, not just quantitative — it is not about knowing more; it is about knowing how things fit together.

---

### Level 0 — Prestructural

The learner has not engaged meaningfully with the task. Responses are irrelevant, confused, or show a fundamental misunderstanding of what is being asked. The learner may have missed the point entirely.

**Characteristic response:** Off-topic, circular, tautological ("Supply chain planning means planning for the supply chain"), or simply incorrect

**Design implication:** This is a diagnostic signal, not a design target. If learners are landing here, foundational prerequisites are missing or the material has significant accessibility issues.

**Prompt example:** "What does safety stock protect against?" → Response: "Safety stock is when you have stock stored safely."

---

### Level 1 — Unistructural

The learner grasps one relevant aspect of the task — one fact, one idea, one step. Understanding exists but is disconnected and incomplete. The learner can define, name, or identify one element but cannot yet connect it to anything.

**Characteristic verbs:** define, identify, name, label, follow simple procedure, recognize, recite

**Characteristic response:** Technically correct but isolated, with no connection to related concepts

**Design implication:** This is the entry-level outcome for any new topic. Foundational modules should move learners through Unistructural quickly with deliberate connection-building activities.

**Prompt example:** "What does safety stock protect against?" → Response: "Safety stock protects against stockouts."

---

### Level 2 — Multistructural

The learner grasps **multiple** relevant aspects of the task but treats them as **separate, additive facts** without understanding their relationships. Understanding has breadth but lacks depth. The learner can list, describe, or enumerate elements but has not yet integrated them into a coherent framework.

**Characteristic verbs:** list, describe, combine, enumerate, compare (surface), match, sequence, perform multi-step algorithm

**Characteristic response:** Several relevant facts correctly stated but not connected — a list, not a model

**Design implication:** Many courses stop here. Learners who complete a course at Multistructural can pass recall-based tests but struggle to apply knowledge in novel situations because they have no relational map of how the pieces connect.

**Prompt example:** "What does safety stock protect against?" → Response: "Safety stock protects against stockouts, demand variability, lead time variability, and supplier disruptions."

---

### Level 3 — Relational

The learner integrates multiple pieces of understanding into a **coherent whole**. Relationships between ideas are understood — cause and effect, trade-offs, system dynamics. The learner can explain how things relate, analyze part-whole relationships, and apply integrated understanding to similar situations.

**Characteristic verbs:** compare (with explanation), analyze, explain why, apply (with reasoning), relate, integrate, classify with justification, distinguish, cause-and-effect reasoning, justify

**Characteristic response:** Demonstrates understanding of how elements interact, not just what they are

**Design implication:** Relational is the target for most practitioner-level learning. A consultant or supply chain planner operating at Relational can troubleshoot within familiar problem types because they understand the system, not just the vocabulary.

**Prompt example:** "What does safety stock protect against?" → Response: "Safety stock creates a buffer against demand and supply uncertainty. The amount needed depends on how variable demand is (higher variability → more safety stock needed) and how variable supplier lead times are (longer and more uncertain lead times → more safety stock). The trade-off is inventory holding cost — more safety stock improves service levels but increases costs, so the optimal level balances those two."

---

### Level 4 — Extended Abstract

The learner transcends the immediate context. Relational understanding is generalized to **new domains, novel problems, or abstract principles**. The learner can hypothesize, predict, reflect on the framework itself, and transfer understanding to contexts they have not encountered before.

**Characteristic verbs:** generalize, hypothesize, theorize, predict (novel context), create (original framework), evaluate (critically), transfer, reflect on the model itself

**Characteristic response:** Goes beyond the task to make original connections, identify principles that apply across contexts, or generate new questions

**Design implication:** Extended Abstract is the target for expert practitioners, researchers, and leadership development. It is rare to achieve in a single course; it typically develops through extended experience combined with reflective practice. Design for it with open-ended projects, cross-domain case studies, and reflective capstones.

**Prompt example:** "What does safety stock protect against?" → Response: "Safety stock is essentially an option — you pay a carrying cost to purchase the right to serve demand that exceeds forecast. The same logic applies to any form of organizational slack (financial reserves, excess capacity, relationship redundancy). The question isn't just 'how much safety stock?' but 'what class of uncertainty are you hedging, and is a buffer the most cost-effective hedge relative to alternatives like supplier diversification, lead time reduction, or demand shaping?'"

## SOLO as a formative assessment lens

SOLO's greatest strength is its use in **formative assessment** — evaluating the quality of learner responses in real time to inform the next instructional step.

Traditional marking schemes reward quantity: how many correct facts did the learner include? SOLO reframes the question: **how well has the learner integrated what they know?**

### Using SOLO to write response quality rubrics

Any assessment prompt can be anchored to SOLO levels to describe what a Unistructural, Multistructural, Relational, and Extended Abstract response looks like for that specific task. This removes subjectivity from evaluating open-ended responses and gives learners a clear developmental target.

Example rubric anchors for a supply chain case study:

| SOLO Level | Rubric descriptor |
|---|---|
| Prestructural | Response does not address the question or demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding |
| Unistructural | Identifies one relevant factor but provides no context or connection |
| Multistructural | Identifies multiple relevant factors but lists them without explaining how they relate |
| Relational | Explains how identified factors interact and makes a reasoned recommendation based on their relationships |
| Extended Abstract | Connects the case to broader principles of uncertainty management; identifies conditions under which the recommendation would change; questions assumptions in the framing of the problem |

## SOLO and constructive alignment

John Biggs later developed **constructive alignment** (*Teaching for Quality Learning at University*, 2003, 2007) — the principle that learning outcomes, teaching activities, and assessment tasks must all be aligned to the same level of understanding. SOLO provides the common scale for that alignment:

- Learning outcome stated at Relational level (explain how X and Y interact)
- Teaching activity designed to build connections (case discussion, systems mapping)
- Assessment task requires Relational response (explain trade-offs, justify a decision)

Misalignment is when the outcome targets Relational understanding but the assessment only requires listing facts (Multistructural). SOLO makes this misalignment visible.

## Comparison to other frameworks

| Dimension | Bloom's Revised | Webb's DOK | SOLO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | Type of cognitive process | Depth of cognitive complexity | Structural quality of understanding in a response |
| Primary use | Writing objectives | Auditing assessment rigor | Evaluating formative responses, writing rubrics |
| Hierarchy | Cognitive process types | Complexity levels | Depth of integration |
| Best for | Objective alignment | Assessment design | Formative feedback, response quality rubrics |
| Works with | Everything | Bloom's (Hess matrix) | Constructive alignment, project-based assessment |

---

## Quick reference

- Five levels: **Prestructural → Unistructural → Multistructural → Relational → Extended Abstract**
- Key distinction: Multistructural to Relational is **qualitative** (more integration), not just quantitative (more facts)
- Use SOLO to evaluate **quality of understanding** in learner responses, not just correctness
- Best tool for writing **formative assessment rubrics** and **constructive alignment** between objectives, activities, and assessments
- Surface learning (levels 1–3) builds breadth; deep learning (levels 4–5) builds integration and transferability
- Target **Relational** for practitioner courses; **Extended Abstract** for expert-level or capstone design
