# Webb's Depth of Knowledge (DOK)

## Origin

Norman L. Webb developed the Depth of Knowledge framework in 1997 while working at the Wisconsin Center for Education Research, with refinements published in *Depth-of-Knowledge Levels for Four Content Areas* (March 28, 2002). DOK was created specifically to help assessment designers evaluate the **cognitive complexity** of tasks and assessment items — the level of thinking required to complete a task, not simply how difficult or time-consuming it is.

Webb's framework emerged partly in response to educational standards reform: as U.S. states adopted college and career-readiness standards requiring deeper learning, educators needed a practical tool to evaluate whether their instruction and assessments were actually building the depth of thinking those standards demanded. DOK has since become a cornerstone of standards-based assessment design and instructional rigor analysis.

---

## The core distinction: complexity vs. difficulty

The most critical concept in DOK, and the one most commonly misunderstood:

> **DOK measures cognitive complexity — the depth of thinking required — not task difficulty or question difficulty.**

A calculation problem involving very large numbers is still DOK Level 1 if it only requires executing a memorized procedure. A simple-looking question like "What would happen if...?" can be DOK Level 3 if it requires strategic thinking and evidence-based justification. Difficulty is about how hard students find it. Complexity is about what kind of thinking the task demands.

Webb (2002): "Each level describes the kind of thinking required by the learning target, not whether or not the task is difficult."

This distinction has a direct implication for learning design: **a course full of hard questions is not the same as a course that builds deep thinking.**

## The four levels

### DOK Level 1 — Recall and Reproduction

The learner retrieves facts, definitions, formulas, or procedures from memory and executes simple, well-practiced tasks. Thinking is automatic or near-automatic; little interpretation or judgment is required.

**Cognitive demand:** Recognition, recall, execution of memorized procedure

**Characteristic verbs:** Define, identify, list, name, recall, recognize, state, label, match, memorize, recite, reproduce, calculate (simple procedure)

**Examples:**
- State the definition of safety stock
- Identify which icon opens the Planning Board
- Name the four steps of the SCOR model
- Calculate re-order point using a given formula

**Assessment fits:** Matching, true/false, fill-in-the-blank, simple MCQ

> **Design Note:** Foundational modules should include Level 1 tasks — learners genuinely need recall before they can think. But if an entire course stays at Level 1, it is building recognition, not capability.

---

### DOK Level 2 — Skills and Concepts

The learner must make decisions about how to approach a task. More than one step is involved; the learner must interpret information, apply a concept to a context, or organize knowledge meaningfully. Some degree of conceptual understanding is required — it is not purely procedural.

**Cognitive demand:** Application of concept, multi-step procedure, inference, classification, comparison

**Characteristic verbs:** Explain, describe, compare, contrast, classify, organize, summarize, apply (in a defined context), use, interpret, construct (from a given template), predict (from a pattern)

**Examples:**
- Compare the impact of increasing safety stock vs. reducing lead time on service level
- Interpret a planning exception and explain what it indicates about current inventory position
- Classify a set of demand signals as seasonal, trend-driven, or stationary
- Organize a list of supply chain events into a cause-and-effect sequence

**Assessment fits:** Short answer, scenario-based MCQ with explanations, classification tasks, data interpretation questions

---

### DOK Level 3 — Strategic Thinking

The learner must engage in complex reasoning. There is rarely a single obvious answer; the learner must develop and justify a plan, construct an argument from evidence, evaluate competing approaches, or solve a non-routine problem. Significant cognitive effort and planning are required.

**Cognitive demand:** Strategic reasoning, justification, evidence-based argument, multi-variable problem solving, design of approach

**Characteristic verbs:** Analyze, evaluate, justify, critique, design, develop, formulate, explain reasoning, investigate, construct an argument, support a claim with evidence, synthesize

**Examples:**
- Analyze the trade-offs of three re-order point strategies given a product with high demand variability and long lead times; recommend one and justify your reasoning
- Evaluate whether the current safety stock policy adequately protects against a 3-week supplier disruption — use the data provided and explain your conclusion
- Develop a demand sensing approach for a product with intermittent demand; explain the signals you would use and why
- Critique a proposed planning policy and identify the assumptions that would make it fail

**Assessment fits:** Case study, scenario with multiple decision points, written justification, decision memo, facilitated discussion requiring evidence-based argumentation

> **Design Note:** DOK Level 3 is the target for most professional certification assessments and for any bootcamp or advanced training that is meant to build judgment, not just knowledge. Scenario-based learning naturally lives at Level 3.

---

### DOK Level 4 — Extended Thinking

The learner engages in sustained higher-order thinking over time — typically extending across sessions, data sources, or contexts. The task requires integrating knowledge from multiple domains, designing original approaches, conducting independent inquiry, or producing work that makes a real contribution. Level 4 is rarely appropriate for standardized assessments and is most at home in project-based or capstone learning.

**Cognitive demand:** Sustained inquiry, complex synthesis across sources, original design, hypothesis generation and testing, cross-disciplinary connection

**Characteristic verbs:** Design (an original system or solution), investigate (an open-ended question), synthesize (across multiple sources), hypothesize, research, produce, evaluate (a complex system over time), create

**Examples:**
- Over four weeks, analyze your organization's planning parameters and propose a redesigned safety stock policy; present findings with supporting data and stakeholder impact analysis
- Design and test a new demand sensing algorithm for a product category with seasonal and promotional effects; document your methodology and results
- Investigate the systemic causes of a recurring planning failure in your organization; synthesize evidence from data, stakeholder interviews, and process documentation; recommend structural changes

**Assessment fits:** Capstone project, portfolio, extended research assignment, simulation run over multiple sessions, real-world implementation with documentation

> **Design Note:** Webb (2002) notes that large-scale standardized assessments should only assess DOK Levels 1–3. Level 4 is appropriate for local or program-level assessment only, where extended time and rich performance data are available.

## DOK vs. Bloom's revised taxonomy

| Dimension | Bloom's Revised | Webb's DOK |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Categorize **type** of cognitive process (what kind of thinking) | Categorize **depth** of cognitive complexity (how deeply must they think) |
| Structure | Hierarchical by process type | Hierarchical by complexity |
| Question framing | What cognitive process does this require? | How deeply must the learner think to complete this? |
| Verb lists | Central to the model | Indicative but not deterministic — context matters more |
| Assessment use | Classify objectives | Evaluate assessment rigor and alignment |

**The key practical difference:** Bloom's "Apply" can be either DOK Level 1 (apply a memorized formula in a familiar context) or DOK Level 2 (apply a concept in a novel context requiring judgment). The same Bloom's verb can span multiple DOK levels, which is why both frameworks are used together rather than as alternatives.

**Hess's Cognitive Rigor Matrix** (Karin K. Hess, 2009) plots Bloom's levels (rows) against DOK levels (columns) — a widely used tool that combines both frameworks into a single assessment design lens.

## DOK applied to learning design

### Audit your assessment rigor

Run this check before finalizing any module assessment:

1. List all assessment items (knowledge checks, lab tasks, exam questions, scenario decisions)
2. Assign a DOK level to each
3. Check the distribution against your course objectives

If 90% of items are DOK Level 1 and your course objective is "Apply," you have a rigor gap. Learners who pass that assessment haven't proven they can do the job — only that they can recall what it involves.

### Build intentional DOK progression

| Module type | DOK target |
|---|---|
| Foundational / onboarding | Primarily Level 1–2 |
| Practitioner / intermediate | Primarily Level 2–3 |
| Advanced / certification | Primarily Level 3 (with Level 4 for capstone) |
| Microlearning knowledge check | Level 1–2 (match to the artifact's objective) |
| Scenario-based learning | Level 3 |
| Project-based capstone | Level 4 |

### DOK and scenario design

Scenario-based learning is inherently a Level 3 format — it places the learner in a non-routine situation requiring judgment, planning, and evidence-based decision-making. The test: if a learner can answer the scenario question by recalling a definition, the scenario is poorly designed. A well-designed scenario forces the learner to weigh options, apply principles under uncertainty, and justify a course of action. That is strategic thinking.

## Quick reference

- DOK measures **cognitive complexity** (depth of thinking required), **not difficulty** (how hard learners find it)
- Four levels: **Recall (1), Skills/Concept (2), Strategic Thinking (3), Extended Thinking (4)**
- A hard calculation at Level 1 is still Level 1; a simple comparison at Level 2 is still Level 2
- Use DOK to **audit assessment rigor** and ensure learning activities match the complexity your objectives demand
- DOK 1–2 for foundational modules; DOK 3 for practitioner and certification-level content; DOK 4 for capstone only
- Use with Bloom's via **Hess's Cognitive Rigor Matrix** for the most precise assessment alignment
- Webb recommends standardized assessments cap at Level 3; Level 4 requires local, extended assessment contexts
