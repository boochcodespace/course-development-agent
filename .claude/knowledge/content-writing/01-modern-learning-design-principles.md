# 2026 Modern Learning Design Principles

These nine principles govern every piece of content this skill produces. They are evidence-anchored, prescriptive, and operational. If a draft fails one of them, it gets rewritten before delivery.

The principles are grounded in instructional design research (Mayer, Clark, Dirksen, Cathy Moore, Kapp, Knowles, Vygotsky, Alley, Malamed) and recent peer-reviewed work (Heliyon 2024, eduresearchjournal 2025, NIH 2024). Where research updates or qualifies a principle, it is noted.

For the full evidence base, format-by-format guidance, and decision rules, see `references/06-research-foundations.md`.

## 1. One Learning Objective Per Artifact

Every Rise lesson, every microlearn, every flip card, every scenario, and every ILT slide cluster serves **one Bloom-anchored performance verb** (Apply, Diagnose, Configure, Decide, Analyze). One objective. One verb.

If a draft contains two verbs, split it into two artifacts. The cost of splitting is low. The cost of muddling two objectives is that learners retain neither.

**Operational rule:** Before writing a lesson or slide cluster, name the single objective and its Bloom verb at the top of the draft. If a section drifts away from that verb, cut it or move it into its own artifact.

**At the format level:**
- ILT decks legitimately cover multiple objectives across the deck. The principle applies at the slide cluster and segment level, not the whole deck.
- Self-paced modules and microlearns enforce one objective per module.

**Bad:**
> Lesson: New Manager Essentials
> Objectives: Run effective 1:1s, give constructive feedback, and write performance reviews.

**Good:**
> Lesson 1 (Conduct): Run an effective 1:1
> Lesson 2 (Deliver): Give constructive feedback
> Lesson 3 (Write): Draft a performance review

Three lessons, three Bloom verbs, three knowledge checks. Each one clean.

*Research: Cathy Moore action mapping; Heliyon 2024 microlearning meta-analysis; assertion-evidence research (Alley/Penn State).*

## 2. Hook in the First 30 Seconds

The first 30 seconds decides whether the learner stays present. Open with a 1-2 sentence hook that names a **specific, concrete situation the role faces**. Not background. Not history. Not "in this module you will learn."

A useful test: delete the first paragraph you write and start with the second.

**Hooks that work:**
- A pointed question the learner cannot answer yet but wants to
- A stat that contradicts intuition
- A scenario the learner recognizes from their own role
- A common mistake or a near-miss
- A stake — what happens if you get this wrong on the job

**Openings that fail:**
- "In this lesson, you will learn..."
- "Welcome to module three..."
- "This course covers..."
- A definition. Save definitions for after the hook.

**At the format level:**
- For ILT, every slide carries a micro-hook in the speaker notes, not just the deck.
- For self-paced, the first screen is the hook, not the first module.
- For microlearns, the first 10-15 seconds names the practitioner pain. Do not spend those seconds on branding or learning objective recitation.

**Bad opening:**
> In this lesson, you will learn how to give constructive feedback to your team.

**Good opening:**
> Your direct report just submitted work that's almost good enough. You can ship it as-is, or you can have the conversation that makes the next one better. The cost of skipping that conversation compounds.

**Good opening (ILT speaker note hook):**
> *Hook:* "Show of hands: who has avoided giving real feedback because the timing felt off? [pause] Today we make sure timing stops being the excuse."

*Research: Sinek opening guidance; SHIFT eLearning; Cathy Moore "throw them in the deep end"; LearnStream first-15-seconds rule.*

## 3. Cognitive Load Discipline

Working memory holds roughly 4 new pieces of information at once. Slides with 12 bullets and lessons with 5 new concepts violate this every day. The fix is chunking, sentence-level discipline, and ruthless cuts.

**Visual layout level:**
- One Rise block introduces at most one new concept
- One ILT slide carries at most ~30 words of body text and one visual evidence element
- New terms get defined the first time they appear, then used confidently

**Sentence level (this is where most writers fail):**
- Average sentence length: 15-20 words. Trim anything over 25.
- Active voice in 90%+ of sentences
- No nested clauses; no double negations; no stacked conditionals
- One idea per paragraph. If you cannot underline the single idea, the paragraph is two paragraphs in one. Split it.

**Bad:**
> Effective feedback can be delivered in many ways and depends on the situation, the relationship, the timing, the medium, the recipient's emotional state, the stakes involved, and the desired outcome of the conversation...

**Good:**
> Good feedback answers one question: what should the person do differently next time? If your feedback doesn't answer that, it's not feedback. It's commentary.

*Research: Mayer's principles (coherence, signaling, redundancy, segmenting); Sweller cognitive load theory; plainlanguage.gov sentence-length norms; NIH 2024 "Death by PowerPoint" study.*

## 4. Immediate Application at the End

Every artifact ends with the learner doing, deciding, or applying something they can act on **within 24 hours of finishing**. Not a recap. Not "you've completed this lesson."

This is the difference between training that gets remembered Monday morning and training that evaporates by Friday.

**Application formats that work:**
- A scenario where the learner has to make a call (with consequence-driven feedback, not "correct/incorrect")
- A "spot the mistake" review of a real-looking artifact
- A decision point with feedback for each choice
- A short task to apply the skill in the actual product
- A reflection prompt tied to the learner's own current work
- A job aid the learner downloads and uses

**Closings that fail:**
- "You've completed this lesson."
- "Click next to continue."
- A summary that just restates the objective.

**Bad closing:**
> You have now completed the lesson on giving feedback. Click next to continue.

**Good closing (Rise scenario):**
> One of your reports just delivered work that's 80% there. You have a 1:1 with them tomorrow. What's the one thing you'll say first, and why? [scenario branches with consequence feedback]

**Good closing (microlearn):**
> Before your next 1:1 this week, write down one piece of feedback you've been holding back. Bring it. Job aid below.

*Research: Knowles' problem-centered principle; Cathy Moore action mapping; Kuhlmann's "what should learners DO differently"; Five Moments of Need framework.*

## 5. Role-Based Learner Context

Generic training treats every learner like a blank slate. Strong training writes to the role with **specificity (role + situation + stake)**.

The outline names the role. The writing makes that role unmistakable. A new manager does not have the same day, the same stakes, or the same vocabulary as an individual contributor or a senior leader. Scenarios, examples, and stakes come from the learner's actual work.

**How this shows up:**
- Examples reference the artifacts and meetings that role actually touches
- Scenarios take place in the systems and conversations the role attends
- Stakes match the role's accountability
- Vocabulary matches what the role already knows. Do not define terms a senior practitioner uses every day.
- Calibrate to the role's Zone of Proximal Development: name what the learner already knows and what is new ("You already know X. This focuses on Y.")

**Calibration by audience:**
- *Individual contributors / end users:* action-first, task-focused, foreground "do this, then this"
- *Managers and team leads:* peer-to-peer, foreground tradeoffs, judgment calls, and people implications
- *Senior leaders / executives:* assume sophistication, foreground strategy, risk, and second-order consequences
- *Customer-facing roles:* assume relationship stakes, foreground conversation flow and recovery moments

**Bad:**
> Imagine a manager has asked you to handle a difficult situation.

**Good (new manager):**
> It's Friday afternoon. Your most senior team member just pushed back on a decision in the team channel. Five other people watched it happen. You have the weekend to figure out how to respond.

*Research: Knowles' relevance principle; Christy Tucker on personas; Vygotsky ZPD; practitioner guides on second-person POV.*

## 6. Gamification Where Applicable

Gamification is not stickers and points for the sake of it. It's introducing decisions, stakes, and feedback loops that make the learning feel like play instead of homework. The strongest gamification primitive at the writing level is **consequence-driven feedback**: instead of "correct/incorrect," show the next beat of the story.

**Mechanics that fit:**
- Decision points with branching consequence feedback
- Scenario stakes ("you have 3 conversations to navigate this morning, here's the first")
- Time pressure that mirrors real time pressure
- Reveal mechanics: flip cards, click-to-reveal, drag-and-drop
- Streaks across knowledge checks (the Duolingo lesson: bite-sized mastery + immediate feedback)

**Where to skip it:**
- High-stakes compliance or safety content where playfulness undermines gravity
- Content where cognitive load is already high
- Pure reference content (cheat sheets, job aids) where the learner is in extraction mode

**Important caveat:** Kapp himself warns that points-and-badges gamification without instructional design integration is empty calories. Gamification is a delivery mechanic; the writer's job is to build the consequence and decision structure that gamification can sit on top of.

If gamification doesn't fit, leave it out. Forced gamification is worse than none.

*Research: Kapp on intrinsic motivation; Duolingo Method; consequence-driven feedback (Tucker, Articulate).*

## 7. Mandatory Visual Anchor

Every Rise lesson and every ILT slide carries at least one **earned visual anchor**: an image, diagram, screenshot, or graphic that holds the concept in place. Decorative imagery is worse than no image. Mayer's coherence principle is clear: extraneous visuals depress learning.

This skill **does not generate the visual**. It describes what the visual should depict, formatted as `**Visual:** [description]`. A designer or the lesson builder produces the actual image.

**Match the visual to the content type:**
- Process with 4+ steps → numbered diagram, not bulleted list
- Comparison → chart or matrix
- Software task → screenshot
- Taxonomy or set → icon set
- Human moment (a story, a stake) → a single photo
- Concept relationship → labeled diagram

**The "earned placement" test:** if removing the visual would not be missed, remove it.

**Bad visual instruction:**
> **Visual:** An image showing the feedback process.

**Good visual instruction:**
> **Visual:** A two-panel diagram. Left panel shows a vague feedback loop (manager → "good job" → IC → no change). Right panel shows a specific feedback loop (manager → behavior + impact + ask → IC → adjustment). Use a brand accent color to highlight the "behavior + impact + ask" element on the right.

*Research: Mayer multimedia, signaling, spatial contiguity; Malamed Visual Design Solutions; assertion-evidence (Alley).*

## 8. Spaced Repetition Awareness

Single-exposure learning fades fast. The empirical retention lift in microlearning research comes substantially from spacing, not just brevity (Adobe 2022: 145% better retention with spaced reinforcement vs. raw microlearning).

As a writer, your job is not to schedule the repetition; it's to **flag** when a concept should be revisited and to write callbacks/forward-links that thread the curriculum.

**How this shows up:**
- When introducing a high-stakes concept, mark it for later reinforcement: `> Spaced repetition note: Revisit this concept in Module 4's scenario.`
- When closing a module, point forward: "You'll see this again in the difficult-conversation scenario in Module 3."
- When opening a module, point back: "Last module, you learned how to structure feedback. Now you'll see what happens when timing is off."
- For microlearns inside a curriculum, include a one-sentence callback to the previous microlearn and a one-sentence forward link.

**Important caveat:** Microlearning and spaced repetition are paired but distinct. Microlearning is the artifact format; spacing is the empirical mechanism. Writing a great microlearn that is never surfaced again at 1 day, 7 days, 30 days produces brevity, not learning.

*Research: Ebbinghaus; 2024 corporate analytics (80% retention at 30 days with spacing vs. 20% without); Heliyon 2024; Akinbolaji et al. 2024.*

## 9. 10-Minute Maximum Completion Time (with format-aware nuance)

For any single **microlearn or microlearning artifact**, total completion time stays under 10 minutes. The empirical sweet spot is 3-7 minutes; the academic outer bound is 5-15 minutes; treat 3-7 as target and 10 as the hard ceiling.

**This principle is format-aware, not absolute.** Self-paced full courses and ILT sessions are explicitly NOT 10-minute artifacts. Inside those larger formats, the 10-minute rule becomes a **segmenting rule**: every screen, every speaker-notes block, every self-paced section should be completable in under 10 minutes of cognitive engagement.

**Format-specific application:**
- *Microlearn:* total artifact ≤ 10 minutes (target 3-7)
- *Self-paced module:* 10-30 minute sweet spot per module; segments inside the module ≤ 10 minutes each
- *ILT segment or topic beat:* ~10 minutes per teaching beat, with natural breakpoints between beats
- *Whole ILT session:* runs 45-180 minutes; the 10-min rule applies to each beat, not the session

**Why 10 minutes (or less):**
- Matches adult sustained attention for unfamiliar content
- Forces cuts to anything that isn't essential to the objective
- Makes the artifact reusable in microlearning, refresher, and just-in-time contexts

**How to enforce it as a writer:**
- Estimate read time at ~200 wpm for Rise text
- Estimate ILT speaker time at ~130 wpm for spoken delivery
- Add ~30 seconds per interactive element
- If you blow the budget, cut. Don't shrink fonts. Don't cram. Cut.

If a single objective genuinely cannot be taught in 10 minutes within its format, that is a signal the objective is two objectives. See Principle 1.

*Research: Heliyon 2024 microlearning systematic review; eduresearchjournal 2025; Articulate, ACM eLearn, and Frontiers 2022 module-length consensus.*

## How These Principles Interact

The principles compound. A lesson with one objective (1), a sharp hook (2), low cognitive load (3), and immediate application (4) will almost always come in under 10 minutes (9) without effort. A lesson that violates 1 or 3 will blow past 10 minutes every time.

When two principles seem to conflict, defer to the principle earlier in the list. A hook (2) that adds cognitive load (3) is fine; the hook earns the attention to spend. A gamification element (6) that adds cognitive load (3) without serving the objective (1) gets cut.

The principles are a checklist, but they're also a philosophy: **trust the learner, respect their time, give them something they can use Monday morning.**
