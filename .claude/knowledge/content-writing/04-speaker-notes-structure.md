# Speaker Notes Structure: Hook → Teach → Check → Transition

ILT speaker notes are the lesson. The slide is the anchor; the instructor's voice is what teaches. This skill writes speaker notes in a fixed four-part structure: **Hook → Teach → Check → Transition.**

This structure is non-negotiable for ILT slides written by this skill. It enforces a real opening, bounded teach time, a built-in checkpoint, and a clean handoff to the next slide. Without it, instructors default to reading the slide aloud, which depresses retention every time (Mayer redundancy principle).

---

## The Speaker Notes Philosophy

Industry consensus from Duarte, Buffalo 7, Ethos3, Microsoft, and Superchart converges on this:

**Speaker notes are talking-point scaffolds, not scripts.**

Long verbatim notes encourage instructors to read aloud, which kills delivery rhythm and triggers the redundancy effect. Notes should give the instructor what they need to deliver the slide well, without writing the words for them.

**Length norm:** 80-150 words per slide (target the lower end, 80-120, for ILT — instructors elaborate organically).

**What good notes contain:**
- The one key takeaway for the slide
- The transition into the next slide
- Key numbers or quotes that must be exact
- Prompts for stories, examples, or activities
- Stage directions in brackets ([pause], [poll], [demo])
- Precise wording only where precision matters (definitions, statistics, quotations, legal language)

**What good notes avoid:**
- Restating the slide
- Full sentences the instructor will be tempted to read
- Jokes that depend on exact phrasing
- Long paragraphs of background the instructor already knows

## The Hook → Teach → Check → Transition Structure

Every ILT slide written by this skill carries speaker notes in this exact structure:

```markdown
**Speaker notes**

*Hook (15-30 sec):*
[How the instructor opens this slide. A question, a story, a stat, or a stake.]

*Teach (1-3 min):*
[What the instructor explains. Plain spoken language, not academic prose. Include any examples, comparisons, or stories the instructor should land. Use stage directions in brackets where helpful.]

*Check (30-60 sec):*
[How the instructor confirms understanding before moving on. A question to the room, a quick poll, a thumbs-up check, a partner share, or a quick task.]

*Transition:*
[One line bridging into the next slide.]
```

Each segment has a job. None are optional.

---

## Segment 1 — Hook (15-30 seconds)

The micro-hook. Every slide needs one, not just the deck (Principle 2 extended).

**The job:** earn the room's attention for this specific slide before teaching anything.

**What works:**
- A pointed question to the room
- A story prompt (a sentence that triggers a real story the instructor should tell)
- A stat the instructor lands cleanly
- A named stake — what happens if you get this wrong on the job
- A "show of hands" or quick poll

**What fails:**
- "On this slide, we'll cover..."
- "As you can see..."
- Reading the slide title aloud
- Throat-clearing of any kind

**Strong hook examples:**

> *Hook (15 sec):* "Show of hands: who has avoided giving real feedback because the timing felt off? [pause] That's the slide we're on."

> *Hook (20 sec):* "I want to tell you about a manager I worked with who let three months of small misses pile up before saying anything. [pause for reaction] By the end of this slide, you'll know exactly how that happens, and how to stop it."

> *Hook (15 sec):* "If you take one thing from this whole session, take this slide. [land it] This is the moment most new managers lose their team."

**Stage directions in hooks:** use brackets to cue the instructor on pacing.
- `[pause]` — let the room sit with the question
- `[poll]` — quick raise of hands or chat poll
- `[story prompt]` — instructor tells a real story here
- `[land it]` — instructor pauses for emphasis after a key sentence

---

## Segment 2 — Teach (1-3 minutes)

**The job:** deliver the lesson the slide is anchoring. The instructor explains, demonstrates, or walks through the concept.

**Format:**
- Talking points and short imperatives, not paragraphs
- Second-person-to-instructor voice ("Walk them through the failure first...")
- Plain spoken language — what the instructor would actually say, condensed to bullet form
- Include comparisons, stories, examples that the instructor should land
- Use brackets for stage directions and instructor cues
- Embed precise wording (in quotation marks) only where it must be exact

**Length:** 80-100 words per slide for the teach segment alone. The whole speaker note across all four segments lands at 80-150 words.

**Strong teach example:**

> *Teach (2 min):*
> 
> [Walk through the diagram] Show them the vague feedback loop on the left. Land the failure: "Good job feels supportive in the moment. It changes nothing."
> 
> [Compare] On the right, specific feedback with behavior, impact, and ask. The lesson: specificity is the active ingredient.
> 
> [Story prompt] If you have a real example of a time vague feedback let a problem compound, tell it here.
> 
> [Precision moment] The exact framework, in their handout: "Behavior. Impact. Ask." Read this one verbatim.

**Weak teach example (do not write like this):**

> *Teach:* In this slide, we are going to discuss the importance of giving specific feedback to your direct reports. Specific feedback is one of the most important tools a manager has, and many managers overlook this in favor of being supportive, which is a common mistake that we will discuss in detail...

The weak example is paragraph prose. The instructor will read it aloud. Retention drops. Rewrite as talking points.

---

## Segment 3 — Check (30-60 seconds)

**The job:** confirm the room got it before moving on. Every slide has a checkpoint.

**Format options:**
- A direct question to the room
- A "turn to your neighbor" share
- A quick thumbs-up / thumbs-down
- A poll (live or chat)
- A quick task or sketch

**The check matches the Bloom's level of the objective.** A Remember-level slide gets a recall check. An Apply-level slide gets a "what would you do" check. An Analyze-level slide gets a diagnosis check.

**Strong check examples:**

> *Check (30 sec):* "Turn to your neighbor. Name one situation where vague feedback would actually be the right call. [time it]"

> *Check (45 sec):* "Quick poll: thumbs up if you've held back feedback because the timing felt off. [pause for hands] Now keep the thumb up if you eventually said it. [pause] Most of you didn't. That's why this slide matters."

> *Check (60 sec):* "Take 60 seconds. Write down one piece of feedback you've been holding for someone on your team. We'll come back to this in the activity."

**Why this matters:** without a checkpoint, the instructor moves on whether the room got it or not. The check is the difference between a teaching moment and a content dump.

---

## Segment 4 — Transition (one line)

**The job:** carry momentum into the next slide.

**Format:** one line, in quotation marks, that the instructor delivers as written or paraphrases.

**Strong transitions:**

> *Transition:* "And that's where the follow-up comes in."

> *Transition:* "We just diagnosed why feedback fails. Next: how to deliver it so it lands."

> *Transition:* "Hold that thought — the next slide is going to test it."

> *Transition (closer):* "We've covered the framework, the timing, and the follow-up. Monday morning, you've got a job aid and a habit."

**Why a written transition matters:** decks lose momentum at slide boundaries. A written transition prevents the dead air where the instructor reads the next slide title aloud while clicking through.

## Variations and Edge Cases

### The First Slide of a Section

The first slide carries a slightly longer hook (the section opener). The hook can run 30-45 seconds because it's setting up the whole section, not just the slide.

### Demo Slides

The teach segment is the demo itself. Use stage directions to cue the instructor through the live actions. Include the "why" the demo is teaching — instructors often run through clicks without landing the lesson.

### Activity Slides

The teach segment is the activity setup. The check segment is the activity debrief. The transition segment carries the bridge to the next concept.

### Closer Slides

The transition segment becomes the close (no next slide to bridge to). It names the 24-hour application (Principle 4).

### Continuation Across Slides

When a single concept spans two slides (a setup slide and a payoff slide), include a cross-reference in the speaker notes:

> *Teach:* [Continued from previous] Keep the customer story going. The resolution lands on the next slide.

---

## The Stake Line: An Optional Top-of-Note Pattern

For high-stakes slides, add a single-sentence professional stake at the very top of the note, before the hook:

> *Professional stake:* This is the slide where new managers either earn their team's trust or lose it.

> *Professional stake:* Get this wrong and the next conversation gets harder, not easier.

Use this sparingly — once or twice per session, on the slides that genuinely carry that weight. Overuse and it loses force.

## Common Speaker Notes Failures to Avoid

- **Paragraph prose.** Notes written as essays. Instructor reads them aloud. Rewrite as talking points.
- **Restating the slide.** Notes that say what the slide already says. Cut it. Notes are for what the slide can't carry.
- **No transition.** Note ends with the teach. Instructor flounders into the next slide. Always include a transition line.
- **Missing check.** No checkpoint. Instructor moves on whether the room got it or not. Always include a check.
- **Over-scripting.** Word-for-word delivery written out. Instructor sounds robotic. Use talking points.
- **Generic stage directions.** "[pause]" everywhere. Use brackets only where the cue genuinely changes delivery.
- **Length blowout.** 300-word notes. Split the slide; the content is too dense for one slide.
- **No professional stake.** High-stakes slide treated like a generic concept slide. Add a stake line.

## Length Targets at a Glance

| Element | Target |
|---|---|
| Hook | 15-30 seconds spoken / ~30-50 words written |
| Teach | 1-3 minutes spoken / ~80-100 words written |
| Check | 30-60 seconds spoken / ~20-30 words written |
| Transition | one line / ~10-15 words |
| **Total per slide** | **~80-150 words** |

If you blow past 150 words, the slide is doing too much. Split it.
