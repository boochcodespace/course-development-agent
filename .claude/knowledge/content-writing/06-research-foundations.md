# Research Foundations and Evidence Base

This file holds the evidence base behind the writing patterns in this skill. The other reference files contain the prescriptive rules; this one contains the *why*. Load it when:

- Justifying a writing decision to a stakeholder, SME, or designer
- Resolving a conflict between practitioner conventions and research evidence
- Adapting the skill's defaults for a new format, audience, or context
- Auditing or revising existing course content against modern instructional design research

Sources span Mayer, Clark, Dirksen, Cathy Moore, Kapp, Malamed, Kuhlmann, Alley (assertion-evidence), Knowles, Vygotsky, plainlanguage.gov, Quality Matters, Duarte, and peer-reviewed work from 2022 forward.

## Top 10 Skill Instruction Primitives

These are the highest-leverage prescriptive rules behind this skill. Each is quotable, actionable, and ready to drop into instruction.

1. **One artifact, one objective, one verb.** Every slide, microlearn, or self-paced screen serves a single Bloom-anchored performance verb (Apply, Diagnose, Configure, Decide). If a draft contains two verbs, split it. *Anchors Principle 1; reinforced by Cathy Moore action mapping and Heliyon 2024 microlearning meta-analysis.*

2. **Lead with the practitioner pain in the first 30 seconds.** Open every artifact with a 1-2 sentence hook that names a specific, concrete situation the role faces. Not background. Not history. Not "in this module you will learn." Delete the first paragraph you write and start with the second. *Anchors Principle 2; Sinek, SHIFT eLearning, Cathy Moore.*

3. **Slides display assertions, not topics.** Every body slide title is a complete sentence stating the slide's main claim, two lines maximum. The body provides visual evidence (chart, diagram, photo, screenshot) that supports the assertion. Bulleted lists are the exception, not the default. *Alley/Penn State assertion-evidence research; reinforces Principles 3 and 7.*

4. **Cap on-slide text at ~30 words; never duplicate the speaker's words on screen.** Mayer's redundancy principle: when narration and visuals carry the message, on-screen text should be minimal labels and signaling cues. *Clark and Mayer, e-Learning and the Science of Instruction, 5th ed.*

5. **Speaker notes are talking-point scaffolds, not scripts.** Target 80-150 words per slide for ILT speaker notes. Use short phrases, stage directions in brackets, key statistics spelled out, and exact wording only where precision matters. Never write paragraphs the instructor will read aloud. *Duarte, Buffalo 7, Ethos3, Superchart consensus.*

6. **Write in second person, conversational register.** Use "you" and "we" rather than "the user" or "one." Mayer's personalization principle shows measurable transfer gains from conversational style. *Clark and Mayer ch. 15.*

7. **Show the consequence, do not announce the lesson.** In any scenario or branching activity, depict what happens when the practitioner makes the choice. Replace "That is incorrect" with the next beat of the story. *Cathy Moore "show, don't tell" toolkit.*

8. **End every artifact with immediate application, not a summary.** The closing screen, slide, or microlearn beat asks the learner to do something on the job within 24 hours. *Anchors Principle 4; Knowles, Kuhlmann.*

9. **Microlearns follow Hook → Teach → Apply → Close in 3-7 minutes.** A microlearn is closer to a print ad with a payoff than to a course. One objective, one decision moment, one closing call to action. *LearnStream MICRO-STRUCTURE; Heliyon 2024; 7taps.*

10. **Every artifact carries a mandatory visual anchor that is referenced, not decorative.** A diagram for processes of 4+ steps, a screenshot for software, a chart for comparisons, an icon set for taxonomies, a single photo for human moments. *Mayer coherence and signaling; Malamed; reinforces Principle 7.*

## 12 Decision Rules (If/Then)

Drop these into any review or revision pass. They're the operational tests behind the principles.

1. **If the content has more than one Bloom verb, then split it into more than one artifact.**
2. **If a slide's title is a topic noun phrase ("Giving Feedback"), then rewrite it as a complete-sentence assertion ("Specific feedback changes behavior; vague feedback feels supportive and changes nothing").**
3. **If a slide is teaching a process with more than four steps, then use a numbered visual diagram rather than a bulleted list.**
4. **If on-slide text exceeds ~30 words, then move the excess into speaker notes or split the slide.**
5. **If a microlearn objective is performance support, then write it as a job aid with imperative steps; if skill-building, then write it as a scenario with one decision and one consequence.**
6. **If a self-paced module exceeds 30 minutes of seat time, then split it into two modules with a clear close on each.**
7. **If a paragraph cannot be reduced to a single underline-able idea, then it is two paragraphs in one and must be split.**
8. **If an example uses a generic role ("a manager"), then replace it with a concrete role plus a concrete situation ("a new manager handling public pushback in a team channel on a Friday afternoon").**
9. **If feedback in a scenario says "Correct" or "Incorrect," then rewrite it as the next beat of the story (consequence-driven feedback).**
10. **If the first 30 seconds of any artifact does not name the practitioner pain, then rewrite the opening; delete the first paragraph and start with the second.**
11. **If a visual would not be missed if removed, then remove it. If it would be missed, then ensure on-page text references it explicitly.**
12. **If the closing screen of a self-paced module or microlearn is a recap, then replace it with a specific application prompt the learner can act on within 24 hours.**

## Domain Findings

### Domain 1: ILT Slide Design

- **Assertion-Evidence (Alley, Penn State).** Replace topic titles with sentence assertions. Support with a single visual evidence element. Empirical studies show better immediate and delayed recall than traditional bullet decks.
- **Mayer's principles applicable to slides:** coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, segmenting. The 2024 NIH "Death by PowerPoint" study found medical lecturers routinely violated these and that adherence correlates with comprehension.
- **Quantitative anchors:** Kawasaki 10/20/30 (10 slides, 20 minutes, 30pt font) for pitch decks, useful as a pressure check. Assertion-Evidence: 2-line max headline, 20 words per minute of narration. Practitioner consensus: keep on-slide text under ~30-40 words.
- **Open or contested:** The 6x6 rule is widely cited but unsupported by Mayer's research. Where they conflict, prioritize Mayer/Alley over 6x6.

### Domain 2: ILT Speaker Notes

- **Talking points beat scripts.** Duarte, Ethos3, Buffalo 7, Superchart, and Microsoft documentation converge: speaker notes should be talking-point scaffolds, not paragraphs to read aloud.
- **Length norms.** Practitioner conventions cluster 80-150 words per slide; pacing calculators note ~130 spoken words per minute. For ILT, where the instructor expands organically, the lower end (80-120) is healthier.
- **What good notes contain:** the one key takeaway, the transition, key numbers or quotes that must be exact, prompts for stories, stage directions in brackets, precise wording where it matters.
- **What good notes avoid:** restating the slide, full sentences to read aloud, jokes that depend on exact phrasing, long paragraphs of background.

### Domain 3: Self-Paced Course Content

- **Personalization principle (Mayer).** Conversational, second-person, polite, and active-voice prose produces measurably higher transfer scores than formal third-person.
- **Chunking and segmenting.** Module length consensus: 15-30 minutes per module, with screens or segments of 2-5 minutes.
- **Discovery and problem-centered design.** Cathy Moore's action mapping and Clark/Mayer's scenario-based eLearning both place practice activity before information dump.
- **Recurring practitioner personas.** Christy Tucker and Articulate converge: name a character, give them a role and a stake, return to them across modules.
- **Completion rates inform writing.** Industry-cited 5-15% completion for self-paced. Levers that increase completion: deadlines, smaller modules, immediate relevance, character continuity, rapid first-screen value.
- **Caveat:** Completion rate statistics are originally from MOOC data. Corporate-internal courses with deadlines and management visibility routinely exceed 80%. Use directionally, not as a baseline.

### Domain 4: Microlearning

- **Optimal length.** Heliyon 2024 (40 studies) and eduresearchjournal 2025 converge on 5-15 minutes empirically; practitioner consensus clusters at 3-7 minutes. Treat 3-7 as target and 10 as hard ceiling.
- **One objective, full stop.** Both academic and practitioner frameworks treat the single-objective rule as non-negotiable.
- **Hook → Teach → Apply → Close.** Working frameworks compress to a hook, a teach, a single apply, and a close.
- **Performance support vs. skill-building.** Mosher and Gottfredson's Five Moments of Need framework distinguishes "apply" microlearns (job aids, decision flows) from "new" microlearns (skill-building with practice).
- **Microlearn writing is closer to print ad than course.** Pacing, single payoff, headline discipline, and a closing call to action mirror direct-response copy.
- **Caveat:** Microlearning's empirical lift comes substantially from spacing, not just brevity. Microlearning + spaced repetition is the working pair.

### Domain 5: Adult Learning Foundations

- **Bloom's verbs drive everything.** Every artifact uses one Bloom verb; for senior practitioners, default to Apply level or higher.
- **Vygotsky's ZPD.** Open with what the learner already knows and name what is new ("You already know X. This focuses on Y.").
- **Knowles' andragogy:**
  - Adults need to know why before what. Open with the practitioner stake.
  - Adults bring experience; respect it ("You have probably seen this fail when...").
  - Adults are problem-centered. Lead with a problem, not a topic.
  - Adults are intrinsically motivated. Use extrinsic framing as reinforcement, not motivation.
- **Cognitive load applied to sentence construction:**
  - Average sentence length: 15-20 words
  - Active voice ≥ 90%
  - No nested clauses, no double negations, no stacked conditionals
- **Dual coding.** Text and image carry complementary information, not duplicate.

### Domain 6: Voice and Tone

- **Authoritative-but-warm at the sentence level:** short declaratives establish authority; conversational connectives ("Here is the catch," "Notice what just happened") create warmth.
- **Plain language standards** (plainlanguage.gov, OPM, USDA, AHRQ, ISO 24495): short sentences, common words, active voice, present tense, defined technical terms.
- **Cut these:** "leverage," "robust," "best-in-class," "world-class," "synergy," "unlock potential," "exciting/amazing/incredible" applied to course content, "dive deep," "let's explore," "in this lesson, we will."
- **Replace with concrete verbs and concrete nouns.** Specific imperatives beat generic instructions every time.
- **Inclusive language:** APA Inclusive Language Guide (2nd ed., 2023) and RWS Global Guide. Person-first, gender-neutral pronouns, geography-neutral examples, no idioms that exclude non-native speakers.
- **Sentence-level patterns to use:**
  - "Here is what changes." (signaling)
  - "Notice that..." (cuing attention)
  - "In your role, this looks like..." (relevance bridge)
  - "The cost of getting this wrong is..." (stake)
- **Patterns to avoid:**
  - "It is important to note that..." (delete; just say it)
  - "As we discussed earlier..." (cross-reference instead)
  - "Best-in-class organizations..." (replace with a specific behavior)
  - "We are going to..." (cut and start with the action)
- **Calibrating tone for different audiences:**
  - Individual contributors / end users: action-first; assume task focus; foreground "do this, then this"
  - Managers and team leads: peer-to-peer; foreground tradeoffs and judgment calls
  - Senior leaders / executives: assume sophistication; foreground strategy, risk, and second-order consequences
  - Customer-facing roles: assume relationship stakes; foreground conversation flow and recovery moments

### Domain 7: Quality and Failure Modes

- **Recognized rubrics:** Quality Matters Higher Ed Rubric (7th ed., 2023); SUNY OSCQR; OLC Quality Scorecard; Cathy Moore's Action Mapping Checklist. The alignment principle (objectives, assessments, materials, activities) is the most cited quality test.
- **Common failure modes:**
  - Objective-content mismatch (objective says "Apply," content teaches "Remember")
  - Telling instead of showing
  - Abstract examples ("Imagine a manager...")
  - Passive constructions
  - Bullet overload
  - Decorative imagery
  - Wall-of-text slides
  - Recap-without-action endings
  - Hedge stacking
- **Draft vs. publication-ready:**
  - Draft: covers the content the SME provided
  - Publication-ready: every artifact has one verb, one hook, one visual, one practice, one close; every sentence passes the 20-word/active/concrete test; every visual earns its placement; every objective is measurable
- **Review tests:**
  - The "If we cut this, would learners notice?" test (Mayer's coherence in operational form)
  - The "Read it aloud" test for self-paced and speaker notes
  - The "First 30 seconds" test
  - The "concrete role" test (replace generic roles with specific role + situation + stake)

### Domain 8: Emerging Practices (2024+)

- **Performance ecosystems over courses.** L&D moving from "courses" to "performance ecosystems" (Gottfredson, Mosher, Adobe 2024). Implication: more job aids, more microlearns, fewer 60-minute self-paced modules.
- **Cathy Moore's "stop being an order taker"** is now mainstream. Every artifact starts with the business outcome, not the SME content dump.
- **Branching scenarios with consequence-driven feedback** are replacing MCQ-with-correct-answer feedback (Tucker, Articulate).
- **Spaced repetition is the empirical lever.** Raw microlearning without spacing produces only modest retention gains (Speach 2024; multiple meta-analyses).
- **AI-assisted writing patterns:** anchor every prompt to a specific learning objective; use rubric-based evaluation; do not let AI write "good-sounding" copy without an instructional design rubric behind it. Working prompt structure: Goal, Role, Task, Requirements.
- **Tech-forward case studies:**
  - GitLab Handbook: 2,000+ pages, plain language, consistent voice. Scale and warmth coexist when style is enforced.
  - Duolingo Method: bite-sized mastery, mini-units, characters with personality, consequence-driven feedback. Transfers to corporate microlearning.
  - Notion, Stripe, Anthropic documentation: short paragraphs, second-person, signaled callouts, paired text/screenshot. Enterprise learning is converging on developer documentation conventions.

## Voice and Tone Reference Library

Eight short examples of authoritative-but-warm learning content writing, annotated.

**Example 1: Cathy Moore, "Show, don't tell" toolkit**
> "Don't describe what people say. Have them actually say it. Imagine you're watching a film. How do you know what a character is thinking or feeling? By what they say and do. No narrator explains it to you. Try to create the same experience for your scenario players."

*What works:* Two-word imperative opens. Second person throughout. Rhetorical question with payoff. Sentence length varies (5, 7, 8, 9, 8, 11 words). No corporate hedging.

**Example 2: Julie Dirksen, Design for How People Learn**
> "Great teachers know their students as well as their topic. Before launching into a lesson plan, find out what your learners actually know, not just what they are supposed to know."

*What works:* Aphoristic opening declarative. Imperative second sentence. The "actually know vs. supposed to know" pattern is the core teaching, embedded in a sentence rather than lectured.

**Example 3: Tom Kuhlmann, Rapid eLearning Blog**
> "We've all been there, building courses where we don't have much say in the content. You know the ones I'm talking about: those compliance courses or those annual refreshers driven by content more than performance."

*What works:* First-person plural establishes solidarity. The "you know the ones" beat is conversational without being false. Concrete nouns: "compliance courses," "annual refreshers."

**Example 4: Connie Malamed, on slide design**
> "When the visual design is poor, when there's a lot of extraneous information, when things aren't aligned, when it's sloppy, it detracts from the learning."

*What works:* Anaphoric "when" structure builds rhythm. The diagnosis ("it detracts from the learning") lands because the buildup earned it.

**Example 5: plainlanguage.gov writing guide**
> "Use 'you' to speak directly to your readers. When you use 'you,' your readers know what the document is asking of them. Be careful, though. Use 'you' to refer to your reader and another word, such as 'we' or the name of your agency, to refer to yourself."

*What works:* Imperative opens. Justification follows. The "be careful, though" is a warm interruption that respects the reader's intelligence. Demonstrates its own rule.

**Example 6: Ruth Clark, e-Learning and the Science of Instruction**
> "Material using a conversational writing style and addition of on-screen characters can be more effective for some types of learner. Instructional text that uses a formal, impersonal, third-person style tends to make the author seem invisible."

*What works:* Authoritative without hedging into uselessness. The "invisible author" image is concrete. Contrast structure (formal then conversational) makes the principle land.

## Caveats and Open Questions

- **Practitioner vs. academic agreement:** The 6x6 PowerPoint rule is widely cited in practitioner literature but lacks empirical support. Where it conflicts with assertion-evidence and Mayer's principles, prioritize Mayer/Alley.
- **Microlearning length range:** Different sources cite 2-5, 3-7, 5-10, and 5-15 minutes. The 3-7 minute range is a defensible practitioner mode; 5-15 is the academic outer bound. Treat 3-7 as target and 10 as hard ceiling.
- **Completion rate statistics:** The 5-15% figure is originally from MOOC data. Corporate-internal courses routinely exceed 80%. Use directionally.
- **Speaker notes length norms:** The 80-150 words per slide range comes from public-speaking and pitch contexts. ILT-specific norms are thinner; the lower end (80-120) is recommended for ILT because instructors elaborate.
- **AI-assisted writing:** The field is moving fast and 2024-2025 case studies are mostly vendor-published. Treat vendor case studies as illustrative, not as evidence base.
- **Voice patterns:** Sentence-level patterns for "authoritative-but-warm" voice are largely codified in practitioner experience rather than peer-reviewed research. The Voice Reference Library above is the strongest available evidence; treat as illustrative best practice rather than empirically validated patterns.
