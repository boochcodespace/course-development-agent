# Bloom's Alignment Cues for Writing

The outline names a Bloom's verb for each learning objective. The writing has to match. Content shaped for "Remember" but tagged "Apply" misses by an entire cognitive level. This file gives writing cues, sample stems, activity matches, and assessment shapes for each Bloom's level.

The rule: **the verb in the objective drives the verbs in the writing, the activities, and the assessment.** That's the alignment test.

---

## Quick Reference

| Bloom's Level | Cognitive Demand | Writing Voice | Activity Type | Assessment Shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Remember | Recall facts | Direct, definitional | Flip cards, matching | Identify, list, name |
| Understand | Explain in own words | Conversational, explanatory | Summarize, paraphrase | Explain why, compare |
| Apply | Use in a new situation | Scenario-driven, second person | Tasks, simulations, "do" prompts | Solve, demonstrate |
| Analyze | Break down, find patterns | Diagnostic, comparative | Decision points, contrast cases | Differentiate, diagnose |
| Evaluate | Judge against criteria | Argumentative, criteria-based | Critique, recommend | Justify, defend |
| Create | Build something new | Generative, open | Build, design, propose | Produce, construct |

---

## Level 1 — Remember

**The cognitive demand:** recall information.

**Writing cues:**
- Direct, definitional sentences
- Mnemonic structures, lists, glossaries
- Visual aids that label and name
- Repetition is acceptable (and useful) at this level

**Sample objective stems:** *List the... / Identify the... / Define... / Name the...*

**Activity matches:**
- Flashcards / flip cards
- Matching exercises
- Labeled diagrams
- Glossary blocks

**Assessment shape:** "Which of the following is the definition of [term]?" (multiple choice, single best answer)

**Writing example:**
> **Visual:** A labeled diagram showing the four phases of a sales cycle.
> A sales cycle has four phases: Discover, Qualify, Propose, Close. Each phase has its own purpose and its own exit criteria. You move forward only when the criteria are met.

---

## Level 2 — Understand

**The cognitive demand:** explain in your own words.

**Writing cues:**
- Conversational, explanatory tone
- Comparisons, analogies, restatements
- Cause-and-effect framing
- Examples that show the concept in context

**Sample objective stems:** *Explain why... / Describe how... / Summarize... / Compare...*

**Activity matches:**
- "Explain it back" prompts
- Compare-and-contrast tables
- Concept maps
- Worked examples with commentary

**Assessment shape:** "In your own words, why does a deal stall when the qualifying phase gets skipped?"

**Writing example:**
> Why does a deal stall when qualifying gets skipped? Imagine showing up to a job interview without knowing what role you applied for. You can talk about yourself all day; none of it lands. Qualifying is the part where you find out what role you're interviewing for. Skip it, and every conversation after is guesswork.

---

## Level 3 — Apply

**The cognitive demand:** use the concept in a new situation.

**Writing cues:**
- Scenario-driven, second person, present tense
- "Here's the situation. What do you do?"
- Role-specific stakes and constraints
- The learner is in the seat, not watching from the side

**Sample objective stems:** *Apply... / Use... / Configure... / Solve... / Implement...*

**Activity matches:**
- Scenarios with one decision and one consequence
- Hands-on tasks in the actual product
- Role-plays
- Simulated workflows

**Assessment shape:** Scenario question with role-specific context. "You're the account executive. The buyer just said 'send me a proposal' on the first call. What do you do, and why?"

**Writing example:**
> It's Tuesday afternoon. You just got off a 20-minute discovery call with a director-level buyer. They ended with: "Just send me a proposal."
>
> First decision: do you send the proposal as requested, or push back and ask for one more conversation? [scenario branches with consequence feedback]

---

## Level 4 — Analyze

**The cognitive demand:** break a concept into parts, see relationships, identify patterns.

**Writing cues:**
- Diagnostic, comparative tone
- "What's different here? What's the same?"
- Multiple variables, multiple data points
- Root-cause framing

**Sample objective stems:** *Differentiate... / Analyze... / Diagnose... / Investigate... / Compare...*

**Activity matches:**
- Decision points with multiple variables
- Side-by-side case comparisons
- Data interpretation exercises
- "Spot the difference" or "spot the cause" activities

**Assessment shape:** Two scenarios side by side. "Both deals stalled at the proposal stage. One is a pricing issue; one is a stakeholder issue. Which is which, and what's the evidence?"

**Writing example:**
> Two account executives are looking at stalled deals.
>
> Deal A: champion is engaged, no economic buyer identified, three weeks of silence.
> Deal B: economic buyer engaged, champion went quiet, procurement just got involved.
>
> Same outcome (stalled). Different cause. Your job is to tell them apart before recommending a next move. [diagnosis activity]

---

## Level 5 — Evaluate

**The cognitive demand:** judge something against criteria.

**Writing cues:**
- Argumentative, criteria-based
- Explicit standards and tradeoffs named
- "Which is better, and why?"
- Comfortable with ambiguity and competing goods

**Sample objective stems:** *Evaluate... / Critique... / Justify... / Recommend... / Defend...*

**Activity matches:**
- Critique an existing artifact
- Defend a recommendation
- Rank options against named criteria
- Stakeholder-style debates

**Assessment shape:** "Here are three onboarding approaches for a new hire on a remote team. Rank them, and justify your ranking using the four criteria from this module."

**Writing example:**
> Three onboarding approaches for a remote new hire:
>
> 1. Full immersion week (cohort-based, synchronous)
> 2. Self-paced curriculum with weekly check-ins
> 3. Buddy-paired shadowing for the first month
>
> Each one is right in some context and wrong in others. Your task is to pick one for a senior individual contributor joining a distributed team, and justify your pick using the four criteria we just covered. [structured critique activity]

---

## Level 6 — Create

**The cognitive demand:** build something new from the parts.

**Writing cues:**
- Generative, open-ended
- Few constraints, named outcomes
- The learner is the author, not the executor
- Comfortable with uncertainty about the right answer

**Sample objective stems:** *Design... / Build... / Construct... / Propose... / Develop...*

**Activity matches:**
- Build a new artifact (a process, a job aid, a workflow)
- Propose a recommendation to a stakeholder
- Design a workflow from scratch
- Capstone projects

**Assessment shape:** "Design an onboarding plan for a new hire joining a fully remote team with no documented playbooks. Submit your plan and your rationale."

**Writing example:**
> Your assignment: design an onboarding plan for a new senior hire joining a fully remote team with no documented playbooks, no buddy program, and a manager with three other reports who are also new.
>
> There is no single right answer. There are better answers and worse answers, and you'll be judged on how you handle the tradeoffs. Submit your plan, your assumptions, and your rationale. [capstone-style prompt]

---

## Alignment Tests

Before delivering content, run these three checks:

1. **Verb alignment:** does the writing demand the same cognitive level as the objective verb? An "Apply" objective with recall-only content fails.
2. **Activity alignment:** does the practice activity match the verb? An "Analyze" objective with multiple-choice recall fails.
3. **Assessment alignment:** does the knowledge check or scenario match the verb? An "Evaluate" objective with a fill-in-the-blank fails.

If any of the three fails, rewrite the failing element until all three match.

---

## A Note on Senior Practitioners

For senior practitioners, default to **Apply level or higher**. Remember-level content for senior audiences reads as condescending and wastes their time. If a senior audience genuinely needs to recall a fact, fold it into a scenario at Apply or Analyze level instead.

This is a Knowles principle in operation: adults learn best when content respects their existing expertise.
